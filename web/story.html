Why it matters

Patient experience is not hospitality language. It is safety, dignity, trust, and survival.

Rosie Bartel is publicly described as a Wisconsin patient advocate and sepsis survivor. Her story begins with a total knee replacement that developed into a MRSA infection. Public profiles describe repeated surgeries, hospitalizations, transfusions, amputations, and repeated episodes of sepsis and septic shock.

This story does not replace research. It explains why the toolkit exists. The patient voice section is about whether a health system listens early, escalates fast, prevents infection, supports families, and turns lived experience into safer care design.

Would this be safe enough for my mother before, during, and after total knee replacement?
Would the patient and family know the infection warning signs that require urgent escalation?
Would staff listen if the patient or family says something is wrong?
Would the PFAC have authority to change the workflow, not just comment on it?
Would this be acceptable if the patient were me or someone I love?

How the story changes the toolkit

Patient-first means every metric has a human consequence.

Before surgery

Prepare families to recognize harm early.

Plain language infection warning signs, escalation instructions, and family confidence to speak up are part of the experience standard.

During care

Listen when something feels wrong.

PFAC-informed workflows should reduce dismissal, fragmentation, confusion, and unsafe handoffs.

After discharge

Do not abandon the patient at the riskiest moment.

The toolkit should connect PFAC input to follow-up, care transitions, symptoms, infection concerns, and family navigation.

System learning

Use lived experience to redesign care.

Stories should become structured learning: themes, owners, actions, due dates, documentation, and visible follow-through.
