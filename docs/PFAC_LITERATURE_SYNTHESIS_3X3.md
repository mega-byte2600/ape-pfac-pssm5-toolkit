# PFAC Literature Synthesis

## Origins: Historical development

PFACs emerged in US pediatric care in the early 1980s, as families sought greater involvement in hospital practices. The 2025 scoping study identifies Boston Children’s Hospital as one of the first hospitals to establish a Patient and Family Advisory Council, in 1982.¹,²

Councils evolved from raising concerns to reviewing institutional proposals and, increasingly, co-designing initiatives from the outset. This progression reflects levels of engagement ranging from consultation to partnership, not a standard attained by every council.¹–³

PFACs became a formal route for patient input into service design and governance. The evidence supports “one of the first,” not a definitive first council; historical examples also show that influence depends on how institutions involve advisors.²,³

## Evidence: Signals and limitations

A scoping study of 143 articles found PFAC roles spanning project approval, feedback and co-design; feedback was most common. Council existence therefore establishes a channel for input, not proof of shared decision-making.²

Systematic reviews found limited high-quality evidence directly linking councils to clinical, safety or experience outcomes. The evidence base remains limited by heterogeneous initiatives, weak outcome measurement and difficulty attributing changes to PFAC activity.⁴,⁵

Representation and measurement remain blind spots. Patients outside councils may not know who represents them; tracking meetings alone cannot establish influence, decisions or follow-through. These are actionable evaluation gaps, not demonstrated patient benefits.¹,⁶

## Opportunities and gaps

Leaders can examine who is represented, when advisors enter decisions and what changes because of their input. An existing engagement evaluation tool assesses design integrity, influence, organizational culture and collaboration, but does not validate a particular PFAC’s effectiveness.⁶,⁷

The APE toolkit translates these gaps into a leadership assessment covering sponsorship, representation, decision involvement, documented action, measurement and report-back. Oliver and colleagues provide a conceptual bridge from patient-reported information to informed action, not proof of PFAC-specific effects.¹,⁸

Storytelling is a proposed toolkit method, not an established intervention: with consent, capture an account, identify a system issue, discuss it through the PFAC, assign an owner, record action, measure and report back. Pair narratives with evidence; evaluate implementation before claiming outcomes.¹,⁸
