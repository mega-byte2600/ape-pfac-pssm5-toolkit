# Geisel Faculty Site Review

Review date: 2026-09-07

Reviewer role: faculty reviewer assessing whether the APE/ILE public site reads as a credible applied public health and health system improvement product for Geisel, The Dartmouth Institute, Dartmouth Health, preceptor review, and external collaborators.

## Faculty-level bottom line

The project has a strong and timely core: PFACs should not be treated as symbolic listening groups. They should function as patient and family engagement infrastructure tied to safety, experience, equity, governance, measurement, and learning health system action.

The current public site is promising, but it still needs one more disciplined faculty-readiness pass. The main weakness is not the idea. The weakness is page discipline. Several pages repeat the same framing, and some pages still read like an evolving build rather than a finished reviewer pathway.

The site should feel like a polished APE/ILE product, not a development environment. The reviewer should understand the project within one minute, verify the evidence pathway within five minutes, and see exactly where Dartmouth Health could use the work.

## Highest-priority faculty concerns

### 1. Page discipline and duplication

Finding: The homepage, Evidence page, Evidence-Based PFAC Summary, Deliverables page, About page, Collaboration page, and Reviewer Readiness page repeat similar content about patient voice, APE/ILE purpose, evidence-to-action logic, Notion, and reviewer flow.

Risk: Repetition makes the site feel less mature. It can read as AI-generated even when the underlying content is good.

Required correction: Assign one job to each page and remove repeated explanations.

Recommended page responsibilities:

| Page | Primary job |
|---|---|
| Homepage | Explain the project and reviewer path within seconds. |
| Evidence-Based PFAC Summary | Preserve the core synthesis and argument. |
| Evidence Launch Page | Route reviewers to summary, bibliography, matrix, research plan, surveillance method, and source map. |
| Research Plan | Document PubMed search lanes, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, and screening workflow. |
| Surveillance Method | Explain the reusable evidence surveillance system. |
| Dartmouth Health Benchmark | Show an honest benchmark assessment framework and evidence status. |
| Evidence Matrix | Convert sources into patient benefit, risk, PFAC action, leadership action, and metrics. |
| HAI/MRSA Risk Alert | Provide a patient-friendly safety use case with PFAC questions. |
| Bibliography | Provide AMA 11 references separated by evidence type. |
| Story Anchor | Acknowledge Rosie Bartel as lived-experience anchor, separate from research evidence. |
| About | Project lead, contact, LinkedIn, email, QR, and one Notion link. |
| Collaboration | Explain Notion intake, contribution rules, and review guardrails only. |

### 2. Reviewer Readiness page should be de-emphasized or renamed

Finding: The former MVP page has been cleaned into a Reviewer Readiness page. That is better, but it may still feel like a meta-page about the site rather than a necessary public deliverable.

Risk: Faculty reviewers may wonder why they are reading a page about readiness instead of the actual work.

Required correction: Keep it only if it serves a clear reviewer route. Otherwise remove it from primary navigation and leave it as an internal public QA page.

Preferred public label: Reviewer Guide.

Avoid: MVP, portal, acceptance criteria, release, regression, internal build, production language.

### 3. Dartmouth Health Benchmark must not overclaim

Finding: The DH Benchmark page is now correctly labeled as a framework rather than a scored maturity model. It separates publicly supported items from not-yet-validated items.

Risk: If this page drifts back to scoring language without data, it becomes overclaiming.

Required correction: Preserve this discipline. Do not score Dartmouth Health until each domain has evidence, finding, limitation, confidence, gap, and recommended action.

Required public language:

The benchmark is a structured assessment framework, not a completed scorecard.

The framework is real as an assessment method. It is not yet real as a scored benchmark based on complete Dartmouth Health data.

### 4. Evidence Summary is the strongest page and should not be diluted

Finding: The Evidence-Based PFAC Summary gives the project its intellectual spine. It connects APE/ILE scope, PSSM 5, PFAC evidence, co-production, Quality 3.0, learning health system action, and the patient-first thesis.

Risk: Over-editing this page could weaken the voice and the argument.

Required correction: Preserve the summary. Only remove one repeated or unnecessary card if needed. Do not rewrite the full synthesis.

### 5. HAI/MRSA page is useful but has one unfinished placeholder

Finding: The HAI/MRSA page contains strong patient-friendly PFAC questions and national HAI framing, but it includes a section labeled as reserved for final synthesis text.

Risk: Placeholders reduce reviewer confidence. Faculty will notice unfinished language.

Required correction: Replace the placeholder with a concise synthesis paragraph that connects HAI/MRSA, post-discharge warning signs, escalation, PFAC agenda-setting, and leadership follow-through.

Suggested replacement concept:

Healthcare-associated infection prevention is not only a technical safety program. For patients and families, it is also a communication, discharge readiness, trust, and escalation problem. PFACs can help leaders test whether infection warning signs are understandable, whether escalation pathways work after discharge, whether patient concerns are believed, and whether harm signals become system learning.

### 6. About page is still too large

Finding: The About page has the right content but too much project explanation.

Risk: A reviewer looking for contact information should not have to parse another project overview.

Required correction: Shorten About by 30 to 40 percent.

Keep:

1. Michael Bolton
2. One concise professional paragraph
3. LinkedIn
4. Email
5. QR code
6. Notion workspace link
7. Minimal reviewer links

Remove or move:

1. Repeated explanation of the full project
2. Repeated Notion/workspace explanation already present on Collaboration
3. Repeated patient-first thesis already present on the homepage and story page

### 7. Collaboration page should be operational, not promotional

Finding: The Collaboration page is close, but it repeats the public site and source-of-record language already seen elsewhere.

Risk: It could feel redundant.

Required correction: Make the page about how to contribute.

Best structure:

1. What the Notion workspace is for
2. Who can contribute
3. What contributors can submit
4. What contributors must not submit
5. What happens after submission
6. Link to Notion and request access

### 8. Bibliography is useful but needs stronger citation assurance

Finding: Bibliography page separates core research, toolkits, public sources, and story resources. That is correct.

Risk: AMA 11 formatting and access dates need a final check. Web-only sources should include publisher, page title, site name when available, and accessed date. Journal references should be validated against PubMed or publisher records.

Required correction: Add a final bibliography validation task. Do not add references that have not been checked.

### 9. Research Plan and Surveillance Method are a real contribution

Finding: These pages are more than assignment compliance. They show a reusable method for literature surveillance and evidence cataloging.

Risk: The Surveillance Method can look too technical if reviewers do not understand its value.

Required correction: Add one plain-language sentence near the top:

This method is the quality control system for keeping the toolkit current after the first evidence review.

### 10. Patient story and research evidence separation is essential

Finding: The Story page properly identifies Rosie Bartel as a lived-experience anchor, not a peer-reviewed evidence substitute.

Risk: The project could lose credibility if story content is treated as research evidence.

Required correction: Preserve the separation everywhere. Do not cite Rosie as evidence of PFAC effectiveness. Use her story to explain urgency, dignity, escalation, and patient harm.
